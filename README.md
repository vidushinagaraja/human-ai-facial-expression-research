# When Human Perception and Artificial Intelligence Diverge

### An Independent Computational Study of Facial-Expression Recognition

This research investigates the extent to which artificial intelligence (AI) facial-expression predictions align with human perceptual judgments, with particular attention to disagreement, predictive uncertainty, and cases where AI systems assign high scores to interpretations that differ from human observers.

The project combines human behavioral annotations from the CREMA-D dataset with predictions generated by a pretrained facial-expression classification model.

An interactive research dashboard presents the methodology, principal findings, statistical analyses, and study limitations.

---

## Research Question

**To what extent do AI facial-expression predictions and confidence patterns align with human perceptual judgments, and when do they diverge?**

## Research Aims

The study investigates three related questions:

1. **Perceptual Agreement:** How frequently do AI facial-expression classifications correspond with the most common judgment among human observers?

2. **Uncertainty Correspondence:** Are facial expressions that generate greater disagreement among human observers also associated with greater uncertainty in AI predictions?

3. **High-Scoring Disagreement:** How frequently does an AI classifier strongly favor an expression category that differs from the human majority judgment?

These aims provide an exploratory framework for comparing human and computational interpretations of facial expressions.

---

## Dataset

The study uses the **CREMA-D (Crowd-Sourced Emotional Multimodal Actors Dataset)**, a dataset containing audiovisual recordings of actors portraying emotional expressions.

The analyzed sample contains:

| Characteristic | Value |
|---|---:|
| Recordings analyzed | 600 |
| Unique actors | 91 |
| Expression categories | 6 |
| Recordings per intended expression | 100 |
| Recordings with a unique human majority judgment | 563 |

The six intended expression categories are:

- Anger
- Disgust
- Fear
- Happiness
- Neutral
- Sadness

Human perceptual judgments were obtained from the dataset's existing visual-expression annotations.

Recordings with tied leading human judgments were excluded from the primary categorical agreement calculation.

**Dataset source:** [CREMA-D repository](https://github.com/CheyneyComputerScience/CREMA-D)

The original recordings and raw annotation files are not redistributed in this repository. Researchers should obtain them from the original source and comply with its applicable terms.

## AI Model

Facial-expression predictions were generated using a pretrained Vision Transformer (ViT) classifier:

**Model:** [trpakov/vit-face-expression](https://huggingface.co/trpakov/vit-face-expression)

The model produces scores across facial-expression categories.

For the primary analysis:

1. A representative frame was extracted from each video.
2. The pretrained classifier generated expression scores.
3. Predictions were mapped to the six expression categories used in the analysis.
4. The highest-scoring category was compared with the human majority judgment.
5. The distribution of model scores was used to calculate predictive entropy.

A supplementary analysis examined predictions from three sampled frames per recording.

**Important:** Model output scores should not be interpreted as validated or calibrated probabilities of an individual's emotional state.

---

## Methodology

### Human Judgments

Human perceptual uncertainty was quantified using Shannon entropy calculated from the distribution of human expression judgments for each recording.

Higher entropy indicates greater disagreement among observers.

Lower entropy indicates that observers tended to select the same expression category.

### AI Predictions

AI predictive uncertainty was estimated using Shannon entropy calculated from the model's expression-score distribution.

Higher entropy indicates that model scores were spread more evenly across categories.

Lower entropy indicates that the model concentrated its scores on fewer categories.

### Human–AI Agreement

Categorical agreement was determined by comparing the AI's highest-scoring expression with the human majority judgment.

Recordings without a unique human majority judgment were excluded from this comparison.

### Statistical Analysis

The study employed:

- Pearson correlation
- Spearman rank correlation
- Actor-aware bootstrap confidence intervals
- Expression-category-adjusted correlation analysis
- Alternative frame-selection analysis
- Expression-specific exploratory comparisons

These analyses examined the strength and robustness of relationships between human perceptual uncertainty and AI predictive uncertainty.

---

## Principal Findings

### 1. Human–AI Agreement

Human majority judgments and AI top-category predictions agreed in **40.7%** of the 563 recordings with a unique human majority judgment.

They disagreed in **59.3%**.

These findings describe correspondence between two classification approaches; they do not independently establish which interpretation is correct.

### 2. High-Scoring Disagreement

Among recordings with a unique human majority judgment, **180 recordings (32.0%)** showed disagreement despite the AI assigning a top-category score of at least 80%.

This demonstrates that a high model output score does not guarantee agreement with human observers.

The threshold refers to the model's output score, not independently validated predictive confidence.

### 3. Human–AI Uncertainty Correspondence

The initial association between human perceptual uncertainty and AI predictive uncertainty was weak:

| Test | Correlation |
|---|---:|
| Pearson | 0.0906 |
| Spearman | 0.1001 |

An actor-aware bootstrap produced a 95% confidence interval of approximately **[0.0014, 0.1758]** for the original Pearson correlation.

Although the observed association was positive, its magnitude was small.

### 4. Robustness Analysis

To evaluate whether the observed association depended on expression categories or frame selection, additional analyses were conducted.

| Analysis | Original Pearson r | Expression-adjusted r |
|---|---:|---:|
| Middle frame | 0.0906 | 0.0283 |
| Three-frame mean | 0.1342 | 0.0198 |

The actor-cluster bootstrap confidence intervals for both expression-adjusted correlations included zero.

These findings provide limited evidence for a consistent association between human disagreement and AI predictive uncertainty after accounting for intended expression category.

### 5. Frame-Selection Sensitivity

AI uncertainty varied across sampled frames within the same video.

The mean within-video normalized entropy range was **0.286**.

This suggests that the choice of video frame can meaningfully influence the model's predictive uncertainty.

---

## Interpretation

The study identifies several important patterns:

1. Human observers and an AI facial-expression classifier frequently assign different expression labels to the same recordings.
2. High AI output scores can coexist with disagreement from the human majority judgment.
3. Human perceptual uncertainty and AI predictive uncertainty are not strongly aligned in the analyzed sample.
4. Some apparent relationships between human and AI uncertainty are sensitive to expression-category adjustment and frame selection.

Together, these findings illustrate the importance of distinguishing machine classification scores from human perceptual consensus.

They also highlight the limitations of treating facial-expression recognition as an unambiguous classification problem.

---

## Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings:

- **Acted expressions:** CREMA-D contains performed emotional expressions rather than spontaneous emotional behavior.
- **Different stimulus formats:** Human observers evaluated visual recordings, whereas the AI classifier primarily evaluated extracted still frames.
- **Model dependence:** Results reflect the behavior of one pretrained classifier and may not generalize to other architectures.
- **Uncalibrated scores:** High model scores do not necessarily represent reliable confidence.
- **Human disagreement:** A majority judgment represents perceptual consensus, not verified emotional ground truth.
- **Repeated actors:** Multiple recordings were obtained from the same actors, motivating actor-aware analyses.
- **Exploratory framing:** The research questions and analyses developed during the project and should not be interpreted as preregistered confirmatory tests.

The study does not attempt to determine participants' internal emotional states.

---

## Why This Research Matters

Automated facial-expression recognition is frequently presented as a method for interpreting human affective behavior.

However, visible facial expressions do not always correspond unambiguously to a single emotional category, and human observers themselves may disagree.

Examining where machine predictions align with or diverge from human judgments can inform research in:

- Human–computer interaction
- Cognitive science
- Affective computing
- Human-centered artificial intelligence
- Model evaluation and interpretability

Understanding these disagreements is important when evaluating the reliability and appropriate use of automated facial-expression classification systems.

## Research Scope

This is an independent exploratory computational research project.

Its objective is to characterize patterns of agreement and divergence between human facial-expression judgments and a pretrained AI classifier.

The findings should not be interpreted as evidence that facial expressions provide definitive measurements of an individual's emotional state.
